Macroscopic dark matter, or macros, covers any dark matter that comes as large composite objects
instead of single particles. Macros would interact with ordinary matter mostly by bumping into it,
so what matters is how heavy they are and how big they are.

A lightning strike is two events. First an ionized channel forms between the cloud and the ground,
and then the current flows down it. Ordinary lightning is jagged because that channel is built in
many short steps, each in a random direction.

A macro that crosses the atmosphere heats the air along its path. If its cross-section is large
enough, it leaves a straight channel of ionized plasma behind it. We work out when that channel is
conductive enough to act like the start of a lightning bolt.

Inside a thunderstorm, a macro's channel could replace all of the leader's steps with one. The bolt
would then follow the macro's path, and it would be far straighter than any lightning we normally
see. Neither wind shear nor magnetohydrodynamic instabilities would bend it much.

<div class="syw-card">
  <TalkSlides slide="q-how-straight-is-straight">
    How straight is straight? Lightning has been made to follow straight ionized channels, from the
    IVY-MIKE nuclear test to laser-guided discharges in the lab. Those tests show what bends such a
    channel, and how little that is over the length of a bolt.
  </TalkSlides>
</div>

We looked for reports of very straight lightning. The best candidate, photographed in Zimbabwe, is
probably not straight enough to have come from a macro.

There are always many thunderstorms going on Earth, so watching for straight lightning is a search
for macros. If none turn up, that rules out part of the mass and cross-section plane, reaching
heavier macros with smaller cross-sections than other proposed searches. Lightning on Jupiter
covers a much larger area, and watching it could reach heavier macros still, though lightning there
is not as well understood.
