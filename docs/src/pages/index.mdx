Macros are dark matter candidates that are large composite objects, not single particles. Many models
make them out of quark matter at about nuclear density. A macro interacts by bumping into things, so
its cross-section is just its geometric size. Dark matter doesn't need a small cross-section: what
matters is the cross-section divided by the mass, so a heavy macro can also be big.

A lightning strike happens in two steps. First an ionized channel grows between the cloud and the
ground. Then the current flows down it, and that's the flash we see. The channel normally grows in
short steps, each in a random direction, which is why lightning is jagged.

A macro moving through the air heats it along its path and leaves a straight trail of ionized gas.
If the macro's cross-section is large enough, the trail holds as much charge as a lightning leader
and lasts long enough for one to follow it.

In a thunderstorm, the leader runs along the macro's trail in one continuous step instead of many
random ones. The bolt then follows the macro's straight path. Wind shear and plasma instabilities
barely bend it.

We looked for reports of very straight lightning. The best one, photographed in Zimbabwe, still
wanders by a few times its own width, which is too much for a macro.

So a search for straight lightning is a search for macros. If years of watching thunderstorms turn
up no straight bolts, that rules out a band of macro masses and cross-sections, including macros at
nuclear density. That needs better recording of lightning than exists now. Jupiter is much bigger
and could reach heavier macros, but its lightning is less well understood.
